A

Tokenization

How text is cut into the pieces a model actually reads, and why the cutting rules matter for cost, numbers and other languages.

A1

Why does the same message cost more tokens in some languages?

Midterm Q4 · Q5

What to notice. Each colored chip is one token. English words are usually one chip each. Spanish, Korean, Hindi and Amharic get chopped into more pieces, because the tokenizer learned its merges mostly from English text. In the numbers example, digits are grouped in chunks of up to 3 from the left, so 13078 becomes 130 | 78, and the space before a number is its own token.

Key terms
Token
The unit a model reads and predicts: a word, part of a word, or a few bytes.
Tokenizer
The fixed rules that split text into tokens and map each one to an ID. This is GPT-4o's tokenizer, o200k_base, with about 200,000 tokens.
UTF-8 byte
How text is stored. English letters take 1 byte; accented letters 2; Korean, Hindi and Amharic characters 3.
Over-segmentation
Splitting a word into many small tokens because the vocabulary has few pieces for that language. It costs more and fills the context window faster.
A2

How does BPE build a vocabulary, and why not make it as big as possible?

Midterm Q1 · Q2 · Q3

What to notice. Each merge adds one token to the vocabulary and makes the corpus shorter. Early merges save many tokens. Late merges create long tokens that appear only a few times: that is the rare-token problem behind Q1. Stop early and words stay split into many pieces, which means longer sequences (Q2). Type an unseen word: it is still built from pieces the model knows (Q3).

Key terms
Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE)
Start from single characters (or bytes) and repeatedly merge the most frequent neighboring pair into a new token.
Vocabulary size |V|
How many distinct tokens exist. Bigger means shorter sequences but rarer tokens and bigger embedding and output layers.
Out-of-vocabulary (OOV)
A word the vocabulary doesn't contain. Subword tokenizers rebuild it from smaller known pieces instead of using one [UNK] token.
Subword
A piece of a word that is a token in its own right, like "er_" or "low".
B

Word embeddings & similarity

How words become vectors, how we measure whether two vectors are similar, and how to check that the vectors mean something.

B1

How do co-occurrence counts turn into word vectors?

Midterm Q6

What to notice. The window slides over every word and adds 1 for each neighbor it sees. Each finished row is that word's vector. "cat" and "dog" are never neighbors, so their cell stays 0. Yet their rows look alike, because both appear next to "the" and "sat". That is the distributional hypothesis at work.

Key terms
Co-occurrence matrix
A |V| × |V| table. Cell (w, c) counts how often word c appears inside the window around word w.
Context window
How far "nearby" reaches. A window of 1 means one word on each side. It never crosses into another document.
Distributional hypothesis
Words that appear in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings.
Sparse vector
A long vector that is mostly zeros. Count rows are sparse; learned embeddings are short and dense.
B2

Why measure similarity with cosine instead of the dot product?

Midterm Q7

What to notice. Drag the arrow tips. Stretching a vector changes the dot product but leaves the cosine unchanged, because cosine only measures direction. Frequent words like "the" have very long count vectors, so the raw dot product would call them similar to everything. In the slide example, "digital" points almost the same way as "information"; "cherry" points somewhere else entirely.

Key terms
Dot product
a·b = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ + …. Large when both vectors are big in the same dimensions, so it grows with length.
Vector length |a|
√(a₁² + a₂² + …), the size of the arrow.
Cosine similarity
a·b ÷ (|a| |b|), the cosine of the angle. 1 means same direction, 0 means unrelated, −1 means opposite.
B3

How does word2vec learn from positive and negative pairs?

Midterm Q8

What to notice. Positive pairs (real neighbors) are pulled toward the same direction, and randomly sampled negative pairs are pushed apart. Press Train: words that share neighbors (apricot, cherry and apple all sit near jam and pie) swing round to point the same way, even though they never appear next to each other.

Key terms
Skip-gram with negative sampling
word2vec's method: train a yes/no classifier on word pairs, then keep its weights as the embeddings.
Positive example
A target word plus a word that really appears inside its context window.
Negative example
A target word plus a random "noise" word drawn from the vocabulary (by frequency) that was not its neighbor. There are k of these per positive.
σ(w·c)
The classifier's probability that c is a real neighbor of w: the sigmoid of their dot product.
B4

How do we test embeddings, and why isn't an average of word vectors a good sentence vector?

Midterm Q9 · Q10

What to notice. If a relation like male → female is a consistent direction, then king − man + woman lands right on queen. That is the analogy test. On the right, averaging static word vectors throws word order away: "dog bit man" and "man bit dog" get the identical average (cosine 1.000). Sentence-embedding models like SBERT are trained so pairs like these end up far apart.

Key terms
Analogy test
Check embeddings with vector arithmetic: b − a + c should be nearest (by cosine) to the right answer.
Mean pooling
Average the vectors of every word in a sentence to get one vector.
Sentence embedding
One vector for a whole sentence. SBERT learns it from sentence pairs, so cosine reflects meaning. It is the right tool for clustering documents.
C

Neural language models

How raw scores become probabilities, how the model is graded and corrected, and why hidden layers need a nonlinearity.

C1

What do softmax and cross-entropy actually do to the logits?

Midterm Q11 · Q12 · Q13

What to notice. Softmax keeps every probability positive and the total at exactly 1, while stretching the gaps between logits. Cross-entropy looks only at the probability of the correct token: near 1 the loss is near 0, and near 0 the loss shoots up. Try "divide by the sum": a negative logit gives a negative "probability", which is why softmax exponentiates first.

Key terms
Logit
A raw, unnormalized score for one token, produced before softmax. It can be any real number.
Softmax
e^z for each token, divided by the sum of all of them. The outputs are positive and add to 1.
Temperature τ
Divide the logits by τ first. Low τ sharpens the distribution; high τ flattens it.
Cross-entropy loss
−log(probability given to the correct token). The training loss of every LLM.
C2

What does the learning rate do in gradient descent?

Midterm Q14

What to notice. Each step moves w downhill by η × slope. A tiny η crawls, and the loss falls very slowly (the symptom in Q14, fixed by raising η). A medium η reaches the bottom in a few steps. If η is too large, each step overshoots further than it started, and training diverges.

Key terms
Gradient
The slope of the loss with respect to a weight. It points uphill.
Gradient descent
Repeat w ← w − η × gradient, stepping downhill on the loss.
Learning rate η
The step size. Too small is slow; too large overshoots or diverges.
C3

Why do hidden layers need a nonlinear activation?

Midterm Q16

What to notice. Slide toward Linear: the four hidden points fall onto one straight line with both "1" points in the middle, so no single straight cut can separate them, and the network gets XOR wrong. ReLU folds negative values up to zero, lifting (0,0) off that line, and a straight cut then works. Without a nonlinearity, any stack of layers collapses into one linear layer.

Key terms
Activation function
A nonlinear function applied after each weighted sum, such as ReLU, sigmoid or tanh.
ReLU
max(0, z): negative values become 0, positive values pass through.
Linearly separable
A single straight line (or flat plane) can split the two classes. XOR's inputs are not.
Hidden layer
A layer between input and output that builds a new representation of the input.
C4

Where do a model's parameters live?

Midterm Q15 · Q17

What to notice. A linear layer has inputs × outputs weights plus one bias per output. ReLU, flatten and softmax add nothing. Push the vocabulary slider up: the embedding and output layers balloon, because both scale with |V|. Turning off "count biases" or "count embedding" reproduces the wrong answers to Q17.

Key terms
Parameter
A number the model learns during training: a weight or a bias.
Embedding matrix
|V| × d. One learned vector (row) per token, with no bias.
Linear(in → out)
in × out weights + out biases.
Softmax regression
z = Wx + b, then softmax. A one-layer, linear language model with one row of W per vocabulary token.
D

Transformers & attention

How each token builds a context-aware vector by attending to other tokens, and how those vectors flow up to the prediction.

D1

Why can't one fixed vector per word capture "it"?

Midterm Q19 · Q20

What to notice. The static word2vec vector for "it" never changes. After attention, "it" becomes a mix of the words it attends to, so it leans toward chicken in one sentence and toward road in the other. In the heatmap, each attention head learns its own pattern. That is the point of multi-head attention: several kinds of relationship tracked at once.

Key terms
Static embedding
One fixed vector per word type, the same in every sentence (word2vec, GloVe).
Contextual embedding
A vector for one occurrence of a word that depends on the words around it. It is the output of the transformer layers.
Attention weight
How much one token draws from another. Each row of weights adds to 1.
Multi-head attention
Several attention heads run side by side, each on a slice of the vector (e.g. 4 × 256 of 1024), and their outputs are concatenated.
D2

What do the query, key and value each do?

Midterm Q18

What to notice. The current token's query is compared with every visible key. Those scores, divided by √dk and softmaxed, decide how much of each value vector goes into the output. Pick x₁ as the query: the later tokens are masked, so it can only attend to itself. Turn off √dk and the weights get more extreme.

Key terms
Query (q = xWQ)
The current token asking "what am I looking for?"
Key (k = xWK)
Each token's label, compared against the query to score relevance.
Value (v = xWV)
The content a token hands over, weighted by attention and summed.
Causal mask
Sets scores for future tokens to −∞, so they get zero weight. GPT-style models only look left.
D3

Why does a transformer need positional embeddings?

Midterm Q22

What to notice. With positions off, "dog bit man" and "man bit dog" give exactly the same output vector for "dog", because attention just mixes a set of vectors and ignores their order. Turn positions on: each input vector now carries where it sits, so the two sentences produce different results.

Key terms
Token embedding
The row of the embedding matrix for this token: what the word is.
Positional embedding
A learned vector for each position (1st, 2nd, …) added to the token embedding: where the word is.
Self-attention
Every token takes a weighted mix of the tokens' value vectors. On its own, it has no sense of order.
D4

What is the residual stream, and what does the LM head do with it?

Midterm Q21 · Q23

What to notice. Each component reads the stream and adds a change back. Nothing is overwritten, so information from the bottom (which word this is) survives to the top. Switch to "replace" and that early information disappears. At the top, the LM head scores the last token's vector against every word's embedding: d numbers in, |V| logits out.

Key terms
Residual stream
One d-dimensional vector per token that flows up through the blocks. Every component reads it and adds to it.
Transformer block
Attention, then a feedforward network (FFN), each with LayerNorm and an add back into the stream.
Language modeling head
Multiplies the last token's final vector (1 × d) by the unembedding (d × |V|) to get one logit per vocabulary token.
E

Shaping model behavior

What each training stage changes, why RLHF holds the model on a leash, when to stop training, and how performance scales.

E1

Training changes the weights. Prompting changes only the context.

Midterm Q24 · Q25 · Q26

What to notice. Each training stage rewrites weights using a different kind of data: raw text, then prompt → answer demonstrations, then chosen-vs-rejected comparisons. Adding few-shot examples also changes the output, but the weight counter stays at 0. That is in-context learning.

Key terms
Pretraining
Next-token prediction on trillions of tokens of raw text. Learns language and world knowledge.
SFT
Supervised fine-tuning on prompt → complete correct answer pairs. Learns to follow instructions.
RLHF
A reward model learns from human preferences (chosen vs. rejected), then RL nudges the model toward higher reward.
In-context learning
Better answers from instructions or examples in the prompt (few-shot), with no weight updates.
E2

Why does RLHF need a KL penalty?

Midterm Q29 · Q30

What to notice. The reward model is only an estimate of what people like, and it overrates answer D (flattery plus keywords). With a small β, the policy piles onto D: the reward-model score climbs while the human rating collapses. That is reward hacking. A larger β keeps the policy close to the reference model, so it improves without drifting into nonsense.

Key terms
Policy
The language model being trained: its probabilities over possible answers.
Reward model
A model trained on preference pairs that scores an answer with one number.
Reference model
A frozen copy of the starting (SFT) model.
KL penalty (β)
Reward minus β × how far the policy has moved from the reference model. It stops the policy drifting away and forgetting.
E3

When should training stop?

Midterm Q27

What to notice. Training loss keeps falling. Validation loss bottoms out and then rises; that turn is overfitting. Keep the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss (epoch 9 in Q27). The curve-fitting panel shows why: a very flexible fit threads every training point but misses new ones.

Key terms
Training loss
Loss on the data the model learns from.
Validation loss
Loss on held-out data the model never trains on. It estimates how well the model works on new data.
Overfitting
Memorizing the training data: training loss falls while validation loss rises.
Early stopping
Stop, or roll back to, the epoch with the lowest validation loss.
E4

What do scaling laws say, and how should a compute budget be split?

Midterm Q28

What to notice. On log-log axes, a power law is a straight line: every 10× more parameters cuts the loss by the same factor. Compute ≈ 6 × N × D, so a fixed budget is a rectangle of fixed area. Chinchilla showed that a taller, thinner rectangle (about 20 tokens per parameter) beats GPT-3's flat shape for the same compute.

Key terms
Scaling law
Loss falls as a power law in model size N, data D and compute C.
N, D, C
N = parameters (not counting embeddings), D = training tokens, C = training FLOPs ≈ 6ND.
Compute-optimal
The best split of a fixed budget between model size and data. Chinchilla found about 20 tokens per parameter.
